# Every Ghanaian SHS Stereotype Explained

## 0:00 · Chapter 1: The question that comes before your name

Thirty years after graduation, a Ghanaian man will forget his wedding anniversary… but he will never forget his school's anthem.

*[Kofa, eyes closed, saluting proudly as an anthem plays]*

In Ghana, the first question isn't "What's your name?" It's: "Which school did you go to?"

Achimota? Ah, so your father has money. PRESEC? Human calculator. Wesley Girls? You have never broken a rule in your life. Opoku Ware? Please… don't fight me.

One answer, and people have already decided your salary, your church, and which school your wife went to.

So today, let's go through them. What Ghanaians say about the big secondary schools, what's actually true, and where it all came from. And stay to the end, because one famous school got its nickname from… Santa Claus. And one small town produced more of Ghana's leaders than any city in the country.

Some of this will make you proud. Some of it will make you call your old classmates to fight. Ei! Let's go back and fetch it.

*[Kofa turns to walk forward while looking back, and bumps into a school gate. CLANG.]*

## 0:45 · Chapter 2: How a Ghanaian gets into SHS

Quick background. In Ghana, at about fifteen, every child writes one big exam: the BECE. Then a computer places you into a Senior High School.

And every parent wants the same thirty or forty schools. The famous ones, the "Category A" schools. Those are the ones we're talking about today.

Now, one rule before we start.

Everybody gets roasted. And everybody gets defended. That's the deal.

If your school is not in this video, it's not because your school isn't great. It's because I'm a small bird and I want to live.

## 1:20 · Chapter 4: Achimota, "Motown"

Achimota School. Everybody calls it Motown.

What do people say? First: they're posh. The children of ministers, the children of big men. Achimota students arrive at school in a V8 and complain the air conditioning was "too cold."

Second: they don't speak Twi. Or Ga. Or anything. Ask them a question in your mother tongue and they'll answer, "Sorry, come again?"

Third: they think they're better than everybody. And honestly? Ask an Achimotan, and they'll tell you that one is not a stereotype. It's a fact.

But here's where it comes from. Achimota opened in 1927, and it was built to be different. One of its founders, Dr. James Kwegyir Aggrey, explained it with a piano.

You can play a tune on only the white keys. You can play a tune on only the black keys. But for real harmony… you need both.

*[Animation: a giant piano, white and black keys lighting up together]*

That's why the school's symbol is black and white keys. In colonial Africa, Achimota was a mixed school: boys and girls, every tribe, every region, learning together. That was radical.

And it worked. Ghanaian heads of state like Jerry John Rawlings came out of Motown.

So posh? Maybe. But chale, that piano was playing harmony before Ghana was even Ghana.

---

## 2:45 · Chapter 5: PRESEC Legon, the human calculators

Next, PRESEC. Presbyterian Boys' Senior High School, Legon.

What do people say? That PRESEC boys don't have friends. They have formulas. That they don't sleep. They just charge. And that if you ask a PRESEC boy how he's doing, he'll say, "Approximately fine."

*[A PRESEC student calculating the angle of his own handshake]*

And where does this one come from? One word: NSMQ. The National Science and Maths Quiz.

In Ghana, this is not a quiz. It's the World Cup. The whole country watches teenagers solve equations on TV, and when a school wins, the students march through town like they won a war.

And PRESEC has won it more times than almost anybody. So the stereotype has receipts.

But here's what people forget. PRESEC didn't start in fancy Legon. It started in 1938 in Odumase-Krobo, a small town in the Eastern Region. The big brains came from the village first.

So human calculators? Yes. But they did the maths from the bottom up.

Now… there's only one school in Ghana that can look PRESEC in the eye at NSMQ. And to find it, we have to go to Kumasi.

---

## 4:05 · Chapter 6: Kumasi, Prempeh vs Opoku Ware

Kumasi has a war. It's not on the news, but it's real. Prempeh College versus Opoku Ware School.

Prempeh, named after the Asantehene Prempeh the Second. People say Prempeh boys are the brains of Kumasi. NSMQ giants. Quiet, serious, maybe a little proud.

Opoku Ware, OWASS. Their name for themselves is Akatakyie: the brave ones. And people say OWASS boys are tough. Loud. If there's noise in Kumasi, check if OWASS is on a trip.

*[Split screen: a Prempeh boy with glasses reading calmly; an OWASS boy beating a drum on top of a bus]*

So in Kumasi, the joke goes: Prempeh has the brains, OWASS has the bravery.

But here's the twist. Prempeh has produced a president: John Agyekum Kufuor. And OWASS? Generations of Ghana's doctors, bishops and business leaders. So the brains and the bravery are on both sides of the road. They just won't admit it.

Hold my egg. Because the next part… is something every single one of you went through.

*[Kofa hands his egg to the camera and rolls up his sleeves]*

## 5:20 · Chapter 7: Things EVERY SHS student knows (mid-video re-hook)

It doesn't matter which school you went to. Every Ghanaian boarding student knows these.

Number one: the chop box. A wooden box, a padlock, and inside it, your whole life. Gari, sugar, Milo, sardines, shito. Lose the key, and you lose your will to live.

Number two: gari soakings. Gari, cold water, sugar, groundnuts. Every SHS student has survived on it. It's breakfast. It's lunch. It's emotional support.

*[Kofa sitting alone on a bunk bed, eating gari soakings with a single tear]*

Number three: "Senior." Every senior was a king. You will fetch his water, wash his plate, and say, "Yes, Senior," even if he's only one year older than you and has never passed a maths test.

Number four: Visiting Day. The most important day of the term. Your mother arrives with jollof, fried chicken, and provisions. And suddenly you have twenty new best friends.

And number five: the one person who always stole your Milo. You know who you are.

By the way, if this took you back, tap subscribe and share this video with the friend who stole your Milo. They know what they did.

*[A suspicious student hiding a Milo tin behind his back, sweating]*

Okay. Now we're going to the girls. And I need to be very careful.

## 6:35 · Chapter 8: Wesley Girls and Holy Child, the holy and the fashionable

First, Wesley Girls' High School in Cape Coast. Everybody calls it Gey Hey.

What do people say? That Gey Hey girls are the most disciplined girls in Ghana. Strict rules, strict prayers, strict everything. A Gey Hey girl will apologise to a chair after bumping into it.

And that Gey Hey parents send their daughters there for one reason: to come back "well-behaved."

Where does it come from? Wesley Girls started in 1836, nearly two hundred years ago, as one of the first girls' schools in West Africa. At a time when many people said girls didn't need school, this one said: they do. And they'll be the best.

So the discipline isn't just rules. It's two centuries of proving a point.

Then there's Holy Child School, also in Cape Coast. People say Holy Child girls are the fashionable ones. Same uniform as everybody, but somehow it looks like a magazine cover.

But Holy Child is also famous for producing serious professionals: lawyers, doctors, leaders. So fashion? Yes. But fashion with a CV.

Now. You've probably noticed something. Wesley Girls: Cape Coast. Holy Child: Cape Coast. And I promised you a small town that produced half of Ghana's leaders…

---

## 8:00 · Chapter 9: Cape Coast, the town of schools (and Santa Claus)

Welcome to Cape Coast. Ghanaians call it the citadel of education.

In one small coastal town you'll find Mfantsipim, Adisadel, St. Augustine's, Wesley Girls, Holy Child, Ghana National College… and more.

So why here? Because Cape Coast was one of the first places in Ghana to meet Europeans. The missionaries came, and the missionaries built schools. Methodists, Anglicans, Catholics: each one built their own. And they competed. That's how one town got all the schools.

First, Mfantsipim, or "Kwabotwe," founded in 1876. People say Mfantsipim boys are proud. Very proud. They'll tell you they invented education, and then correct your English.

But they do have a case. Mfantsipim produced Kofi Annan, Secretary-General of the United Nations and winner of the Nobel Peace Prize. When your old boy ran the world, you're allowed to be a little proud.

Then St. Augustine's, Augusco. The Catholic boys. People say they're the cool, calm ones of Cape Coast. Less shouting, more style.

And now, as promised… Adisadel College.

Adisadel students are called Santaclausians. Why? Because when the school started in 1910, it was called St. Nicholas Grammar School. And who is St. Nicholas? Santa Claus.

*[Kofa in a Santa hat, ringing a bell, very confused]*

So somewhere in Cape Coast, there are grown men, lawyers and judges, who are officially… Santa's boys.

So yes, Cape Coast is proud. But when one small town educates a whole country, you let it be proud.

---

## 10:00 · Chapter 10: The ones people underrate

Now let's be fair, because Ghana doesn't end at Cape Coast and Legon.

Accra Academy, nicknamed "Bleoo." In Ga, that means slowly. People joke that Academy boys take their time. But the full idea is: slowly… but surely. And that's exactly how they keep winning.

And up north, schools like Tamale SHS and Ghana Secondary School in Tamale. People in the south sometimes forget them. But here's a fact: former President John Dramani Mahama went to Ghana Secondary School, Tamale. Not Legon. Not Cape Coast. Tamale.

So the next time somebody says the north doesn't produce leaders… hand them this video.

---

## 10:50 · Chapter 11: Why "Which school?" really matters

Now, here's the serious part.

In Ghana, your old school isn't just a memory. It's a network. Old boys hire old boys. Old girls help old girls. In some offices, the first question in the interview isn't about your degree. It's about your school.

And that's why parents fight so hard for those thirty or forty schools.

In 2017, Ghana introduced Free SHS. Suddenly, hundreds of thousands more children could go to secondary school. So many, that the government had to split students into tracks just to fit them all.

That's a good thing. But it means the big question is changing. It's not only "Which school did you go to?" anymore. More and more, it's "Did you get the chance to go at all?"

So laugh at the stereotypes. Roast your rivals. But remember: the school gives you a crest. You give the crest its name.

*[Kofa holding his golden egg up; it glows]*

## 11:50 · Chapter 12: One anthem

Motown, PRESEC, Prempeh, OWASS, Gey Hey, Holy Child, Mfantsipim, Augusco, Adisadel, Academy, Tamale, and hundreds more.

Different crests. Different anthems. Same gari soakings.

Remember the man who forgot his wedding anniversary but never his school anthem? Now you know why. Because that anthem isn't about a school. It's about who he was when he was fifteen, far from home, eating gari, and becoming somebody.

*[Montage: students from all schools, in different uniforms, singing; Kofa wiping a tear]*

Now I couldn't fit every school. So tell me: which school did you go to? And what do people say about it? Drop it in the comments. Defend your school with your chest.

Now you know where it came from. Go back, fetch it, come again.

*[Kofa turns to leave, walks forward while looking back, and bumps into the end screen]*
